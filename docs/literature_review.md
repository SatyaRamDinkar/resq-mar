## 1. Introduction

The global impact of natural hazards has reached unprecedented levels, driven largely by anthropogenic climate change and escalating urban density in vulnerable regions. The United Nations Office for Disaster Risk Reduction (UNDRR) continuously reports a dramatic increase in the frequency, severity, and compounding nature of climate-related disasters, which routinely overwhelm existing emergency infrastructure. During these crises, the operational "last mile" of disaster management—the critical phase where fragmented citizen distress signals must be transformed into coordinated, life-saving field actions—remains a profound bottleneck. Traditional response frameworks often rely on centralized command centers and static standard operating procedures (SOPs), which frequently buckle under the pressure of dynamic, rapidly evolving crisis environments. Consequently, the central research question guiding this review is: How can multi-agent AI systems improve the speed, accuracy, and resilience of emergency response?

To answer this question, a rigorous review of contemporary literature is necessary. The landscape of artificial intelligence in disaster management is rapidly evolving, with seminal shifts occurring specifically between 2025 and 2026. The transition from predictive machine learning to generative, **agentic AI** models has redefined the boundaries of autonomous task planning, resource allocation, and decentralized intelligence. This literature review synthesizes recent breakthroughs to establish the theoretical and technical foundation for ResQ-MAR. 

This review is organized into four themes: (1) multi-agent coordination in disaster management, exploring the shift from monolithic systems to collaborative human-machine teams; (2) intelligent task planning with Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG), focusing on multi-source data synthesis; (3) adaptive resource routing, examining the convergence of truck-drone logistics and event-triggered optimization; and (4) resilient edge deployment, analyzing the necessity of offline AI capabilities during infrastructure collapse. Finally, we identify the research gap that ResQ-MAR addresses, positioning this project as a necessary evolution in the field of humanitarian informatics.

## 2. Theme 1: Multi-Agent Coordination in Disaster Management

In the immediate aftermath of a catastrophic event, centralized disaster management systems frequently fail due to information overload, communication breakdowns, and the inability of a single coordinating body to process highly heterogeneous data streams. Historically, humanitarian operations relied heavily on rigid, top-down hierarchies. However, as crises grow more complex, researchers have increasingly advocated for decentralized, **multi-agent systems (MAS)**. Early foundational work by Ramchurn et al. (2016) demonstrated that disaster response could be vastly improved by employing human-agent collectives, where software agents autonomously handle data routing while humans manage high-stakes decisions. 

Recently, the integration of Large Language Models (LLMs) has elevated MAS from simple rule-based algorithms to advanced, reasoning-capable agentic frameworks. Li et al. (2025) articulate a transformative vision for this domain, arguing that traditional disaster software is plagued by fragmented data, disconnected technologies, and the loss of institutional memory. To resolve this, they propose the "Disaster Copilot" paradigm, advocating for "collective human-machine intelligence." This concept posits that AI should not replace dispatchers but must operate as a collaborative partner. In their architectural vision, a central orchestrator delegates tasks to specialized sub-agents handling risk analytics, situational awareness, and impact assessment, all while remaining under human supervision.

This theoretical vision is effectively operationalized by Aththanayake et al. (2026) in the ResQConnect platform. Moving beyond the abstract, ResQConnect deploys a tangible multi-agent architecture specifically designed to process unstructured citizen help requests. The system utilizes four specialized agents: a Meta Node, an Assessor Node, a Reformulator Node, and a Task Generator Node. The researchers explicitly avoid chaotic, unconstrained agent-to-agent chatter. Instead, they enforce a deterministic execution order where agents communicate via structured state-passing. As Aththanayake et al. (2026) note, "Traditional rule-based decision-support tools lack the ability to decompose noisy, multi-faceted help requests into sub-tasks, motivating an agentic AI approach." 

The progression in the literature is clear: the field has moved from centralized command structures to distributed software agents, and now to agentic LLM systems operating with explicit human oversight. The integration of specialized agents allows complex problems to be modularized, ensuring that specific models evaluate safety while others synthesize tasks. Furthermore, Li et al. (2025) argue that these systems establish "operational resilience in resource-constrained environments" by acting as an active digital twin that preserves institutional knowledge. However, existing multi-agent platforms remain limited in their ability to operate without reliance on proprietary cloud infrastructure and rarely support fully decentralized, open-source execution—a critical vulnerability during widespread infrastructure collapse.

## 3. Theme 2: Intelligent Task Planning with RAG

A core challenge in disaster response is that traditional, manual SOP-based planning is inherently static and unacceptably slow during a fast-moving crisis. Dispatchers must cross-reference massive volumes of regulatory guidelines, hazard manuals, and real-time incident reports to generate safe operational tasks. To automate this, recent research has turned to **Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG)**, which grounds LLM outputs in authoritative external knowledge bases, thereby mitigating hallucinations and ensuring factual accuracy.

The complexity of disaster environments requires more than simple semantic search. Li et al. (2026) highlight this in their development of DisastRAG, a multi-source integration system. They identify that critical information is invariably siloed across incompatible formats, including structured relational databases (inventory), unstructured institutional documents (SOPs), and dynamic external web data (news alerts). Single-path RAG systems typically fail because they only parse unstructured text. DisastRAG introduces a multi-path routing architecture where a strategy router agent dynamically directs queries to a vector database, a text-to-SQL engine, or a web search API. As Li et al. (2026) report, their multi-source approach achieved "multiple-choice accuracy gains of 12–23% and open-ended keypoint coverage improvements of up to +10.5%" over naive RAG implementations.

Building upon the necessity of intelligent retrieval, Aththanayake et al. (2026) advance the concept of **agentic RAG** in ResQConnect. While DisastRAG focuses heavily on information retrieval and fusion, ResQConnect focuses on the downstream synthesis of that information into actionable, field-ready tasks. The ResQConnect pipeline utilizes an Assessor Node to evaluate the contextual adequacy of retrieved chunks against safety guidelines. If the retrieved SOPs are deemed insufficient, a Reformulator Node rewrites the query to pull better data. Aththanayake et al. (2026) emphasize that "Agentic RAG models treat retrieval and generation as a coordinated multi-agent process rather than a single pipeline," which is essential because retrieving operationally inappropriate data can have lethal consequences.

Despite these advancements, a significant gap remains. Neither DisastRAG nor ResQConnect fully addresses comprehensive multi-hazard scenarios, as ResQConnect was explicitly constrained to floods and landslides. Furthermore, the multi-source integration seen in DisastRAG relies heavily on continuous cloud connectivity to parse text-to-SQL logic and web fallbacks, rendering the system fragile during offline scenarios. While RAG-based planning has shown immense promise in standardizing and accelerating emergency workflows, the literature lacks a framework that unifies multi-source data fusion with completely offline, edge-capable execution.

## 4. Theme 3: Adaptive Resource Routing

Once intelligent task plans are generated, the physical distribution of aid becomes the primary logistical bottleneck in last-mile disaster response. As noted by foundational studies in humanitarian logistics (Holguín-Veras et al., 2012), post-disaster routing is fundamentally different from commercial routing due to the primacy of deprivation costs, extreme demand surges, and destroyed road networks. Consequently, modern research focuses heavily on adaptive and collaborative routing frameworks.

A major breakthrough in overcoming infrastructure destruction is the integration of unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs) with traditional ground transport. Peng et al. (2025) address this by proposing a truck-drone collaborative routing model. In scenarios where roads are blocked by debris, trucks act as mobile depots, launching drones to complete the final delivery to isolated nodes. To manage the immense complexity of this dynamic environment, Peng et al. (2025) employ a Multi-Agent Deep Reinforcement Learning (MADRL) algorithm. Their event-driven Markov game allows agents to learn collaborative routing policies that significantly outperform traditional truck-only systems in reaching isolated populations faster. However, as is common with DRL, the approach operates as a "black box," demanding massive computational resources for retraining and offering zero explainability for human dispatchers.

In contrast, Aththanayake et al. (2026) tackle dynamic resource allocation in ResQConnect using an **Adaptive Event-Triggered (AET)** routing mechanism paired with a deterministic solver (Google OR-Tools). They argue that re-optimizing routes every time a new request arrives creates operational chaos. As they observe, "Triggering re-optimization at every event (continuous re-solving) can yield strong solutions but leads to high computational cost and excessive 'nervousness'." Instead, their AET algorithm calculates a disruption score based on urgency and spatial deviation, invoking the solver only when the threshold is breached. This approach reduced solver calls by 81% while maintaining near-optimal response times.

Comparing the two approaches highlights a profound trade-off in the literature: optimality versus interpretability versus computational cost. Peng's DRL offers highly dynamic, learned adaptability for complex truck-drone fleets but sacrifices explainability and edge-deployment feasibility. Conversely, ResQConnect's AET routing offers mathematical determinism, explainability, and low computational overhead, but completely ignores the necessity of aerial drone collaboration for unreachable nodes. Current routing solutions are either highly collaborative but unexplainable (DRL), or highly explainable but strictly ground-bound. There is no existing system that combines the physical reach of truck-drone collaboration with the explainable, low-overhead efficiency of event-triggered deterministic optimization.

## 5. Theme 4: Resilient Edge Deployment

In the wake of severe natural disasters, telecommunications infrastructure is often the first casualty. Cell towers lose power, fiber optic cables are severed, and entire regions are plunged into digital darkness. Consequently, any disaster management platform that relies exclusively on cloud-hosted APIs becomes immediately obsolete exactly when it is needed most. This "last mile" connectivity problem dictates that field responders and command centers must have access to intelligent guidance even when isolated from the global internet.

To combat this, recent literature has pivoted toward resilient edge deployment, specifically focusing on Small Language Models (SLMs). Aththanayake et al. (2026) integrate this into ResQConnect by deploying a highly compressed, 0.5-billion parameter model directly onto commodity smartphones. By utilizing dynamic-range and 4-bit weight quantization, the model operates natively without cloud offloading. The authors correctly assert that "The edge-deployed LLM is a core reliability layer within ResQConnect, ensuring uninterrupted assistance during periods of low or zero connectivity which is a frequent condition in disaster situations." The edge model is bounded to providing procedural safety guidance, mitigating the risk of hallucination while ensuring victims receive baseline support.

However, the challenge of edge deployment extends beyond simply shrinking a model. While modern SLMs are computationally efficient, they frequently lack deep, domain-specific disaster knowledge out-of-the-box. Furthermore, static edge models cannot adapt to rapidly changing situational data unless they reconnect to the cloud for updates. The literature indicates that while deploying models to the edge is technically feasible, maintaining synchronization between an isolated edge device and a central command center's evolving knowledge base remains a largely unsolved problem. Edge AI for disaster response remains a promising but fragmented domain, requiring architectures that can support fully localized, open-weights execution at the command center level, ensuring that the primary agentic RAG and routing engines never go offline.

## 6. Research Gap & Opportunity

Despite significant advances in multi-agent coordination, agentic task planning, adaptive resource routing, and resilient edge deployment, no existing system integrates all four capabilities into a unified, open-source, and fully offline-capable platform. The contemporary literature presents a fragmented landscape where each advanced capability is developed in isolation, often relying on restrictive methodologies or proprietary technologies that hinder real-world, localized deployment.

A critical synthesis of the literature reveals five specific, intersecting gaps that remain unaddressed:

First, there is a persistent **single-hazard focus**. Platforms like ResQConnect are explicitly curated and tested solely for floods and landslides. Disasters are increasingly compounding (e.g., an earthquake causing a tsunami, leading to industrial fires), requiring platforms that can dynamically retrieve and synthesize multi-hazard SOPs simultaneously. ResQ-MAR addresses this by expanding the underlying vector knowledge base to support a comprehensive multi-hazard scope, enabling agents to dynamically cross-reference procedures for earthquakes, fires, and extreme weather events within a single pipeline.

Second, the literature exhibits a dangerous **cloud dependency**. The most advanced agentic RAG frameworks, including ResQConnect, rely on proprietary, cloud-hosted models like GPT-4o to execute complex reasoning. If an Emergency Operations Center (EOC) loses internet access, the entire orchestration layer fails. ResQ-MAR addresses this by utilizing a 100% local, open-weights LLM stack (e.g., Llama 3.1 via Ollama). This guarantees that the core orchestration, planning, and evaluation agents can function entirely offline, securing absolute operational resilience and data privacy.

Third, current explainable routing engines suffer from **ground-only limitations**. While Peng et al. (2025) proved the necessity of truck-drone collaborative routing for unreachable nodes, they utilized a black-box DRL approach that lacks the explainability required by human dispatchers. Conversely, ResQConnect utilizes explainable deterministic routing but ignores drone logistics. ResQ-MAR addresses this by merging the two concepts: implementing a truck-drone collaborative routing model using a deterministic, explainable solver (Google OR-Tools) augmented with an Adaptive Event-Triggered (AET) heuristic, ensuring optimal reachability without sacrificing interpretability.

Fourth, agentic platforms frequently suffer from **limited data sources**. While DisastRAG demonstrated the superiority of multi-source retrieval, most task-generation platforms still rely purely on unstructured text databases. ResQ-MAR addresses this by empowering its orchestrator agent with multi-source tool-calling capabilities. The system dynamically fuses unstructured vector data (SOPs via ChromaDB) with structured relational data (live SQLite inventory databases), generating task plans that perfectly align theoretical guidelines with actual physical resource constraints.

Fifth, the overarching vision of "collective human-machine intelligence" proposed by Li et al. (2025) remains largely theoretical in end-to-end routing platforms. Existing systems relegate humans to the role of end-users rather than active co-pilots. ResQ-MAR addresses this by wrapping the entire multi-agent and routing backend in an interactive, human-in-the-loop Streamlit dashboard. Dispatchers do not just receive a final plan; they visually inspect the AI's reasoning, manually override routing constraints, and explicitly approve agentic decisions before deployment.

ResQ-MAR occupies the intersection of multi-agent coordination, agentic task planning, adaptive truck-drone routing, and edge resilience—addressing a critical gap in the current landscape by delivering a fully local, open-source, human-centric disaster copilot.

## 7. Conclusion

The integration of artificial intelligence into disaster management has evolved from passive predictive modeling to dynamic, agentic orchestration. This review explored four pivotal themes in the current literature: the shift toward multi-agent coordination and human-machine teaming, the use of RAG for intelligent, context-aware task planning, the necessity of adaptive routing algorithms for last-mile logistics, and the critical importance of resilient edge deployment during network failures. While recent studies have made profound advancements in these individual areas, a glaring void remains. Existing solutions rely heavily on proprietary cloud infrastructure, black-box routing algorithms, or single-source data retrieval, rendering them fragile and opaque during compounding, real-world crises. 

ResQ-MAR directly addresses these systemic vulnerabilities. By fusing a completely local, open-source multi-agent LLM stack with explainable truck-drone routing and multi-source data integration, the project actualizes the vision of a resilient, collective human-machine disaster copilot.
